The Qualification

Three qualifications.
Almost nobody has all three.

  1. Board credibility. At board level this is a capital allocation and risk conversation, not a technology one. Whoever holds the seat has to say no to a CEO, defend a position to an audit committee and CIO, and be believed by people who have been in business longer than the technology has existed.
  2. Operating experience. Strategy that has never survived a payroll run is decoration. The person setting your AI agenda should have carried a P&L, done a turnaround, and watched a good plan break against real staff, real systems and real procurement.
  3. Genuine current exposure. The one the market fails hardest. Most advisers have read about the models. Very few use them over ten hours a day, across every major lab, on real commercial work, and know where each falls over this month. Knowledge here has a half-life measured in weeks.

Consultancies bring the first two and outsource the third. Technologists bring the third and are not in the room for the first. We are unusual in being able to evidence all three, in writing, below.

Currency

Most AI expertise goes stale in a quarter.
Ours cannot.

This is the quiet failure of every permanent Chief AI Officer hire and most advisory retainers. Somebody arrives genuinely current, and eighteen months later they are defending the architecture they chose on arrival, because nobody has time to keep re-learning a field that reprices itself every few weeks.

We are structurally protected from that, and not by discipline. Every two weeks we stand in front of a new room of senior executives and get interrogated for two days by people who do not care what we said last time. The material gets rebuilt after every course. That is not marketing. It is the most demanding continuing-education regime we could have designed for ourselves, and it is the strongest argument for renting this capability rather than hiring it. You are not buying a person's current knowledge. You are buying a position forced to stay current.

The Mandate

What we do once we are in

A CAiO mandate is a standing seat, not a project. The shape below is typical, adjusted to how your board and executive committee already run.

Direction

The AI agenda, with real economics

What to build, what to (not) buy, what to leave alone and what to deliberately wait on. Each with a number attached and a reason you can defend to a board.

Triage

Use-case assessment before spend

Risk, feasibility and honest value, run against the failure patterns we have watched 800 executives hit. Most organisations are carrying two or three initiatives that should be stopped.

Vendors

Platform and licence decisions

Which subscriptions are worth the spend, where a second tool pays for itself, and where an enterprise agreement is buying you very little, from a firm that holds no reseller relationship of any kind.

Risk

Data, security and the policy position

What should never be uploaded, what to redact, which settings to change, which jurisdictions to keep off company work, and a progressive policy your auditors will pass.

Capability

Leadership and workforce capability

Who needs to be fluent, who needs to be merely literate, and what that costs. The Masterclass is the usual instrument, run privately for your leadership team.

Operating Model

Where AI work lives and who owns it

Roles, review gates, and how company-owned methods get built, governed and improved instead of living in one person's private chat history.

Cadence

A standing board and exec slot

Regular decision support for the CEO, the executive committee and the board, plus availability between sessions for the decisions that will not wait for a calendar.

Delivery

Build capacity when you need it

When the agenda calls for something to be built, our forward-deployed engineering practice can do it, in your environment, with your team, as a separate engagement and never an assumed one.
